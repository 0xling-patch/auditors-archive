### Abstract: The Seventy-Second Traffic Light

At the intersection on Zhongshan South Road — the route students from Chonghongdao Junior High take after school — three lanes stretch across the way. The traffic light counts down its final seconds; some rush, some wait. Seventy seconds is just enough for most to cross; for those who start late, it's just enough to leave them stranded in the middle of the road.

### Clues: Why It Feels Off

This is a detail observed repeatedly — not a one-off delay, but a structural asymmetry. Three lanes of road paired with seventy seconds of pedestrian crossing time turns "on time" into a matter of luck.

Students from Chonghongdao Junior High, parents pushing strollers, elderly walkers with slower paces — they all share the same countdown timer. When it hits zero and the green flips to yellow, those still in the middle of the road face not just the imminent red signal, but the direction of oncoming headlights.

The danger doesn't come from a single flaw. It comes from the gap between design and the people who use it.

### Technical Analysis: Stripping the Story Back to Reality

Pedestrian signal timing is typically calculated from road width, design speed, and average pedestrian walking speed. A seventy-second setting may be reasonable under some average scenario — but average scenarios never account for the person who started late.

If this observation holds, the problem isn't that the traffic light is "broken." It's that it serves only those who arrive on time. When an intersection design leaves no buffer for different walking speeds or different departure times, the safety margin compresses down to exactly seventy seconds.

This isn't a technical failure. It's the natural result of a design boundary being reached.

### Auditor's Notes: Next Steps

This observation points to a concrete maintenance issue: the reasonableness of pedestrian signal duration at this intersection. If you've also noticed a similar sense of pressure at this crossing, record the actual time it takes to get across, observe which groups are most often caught in difficulty, and report it to the local traffic authority.

Seventy seconds isn't long — but it's enough to force a choice: dash across, or wait for the next cycle. And for some, waiting for that next cycle is already a heavy cost.
