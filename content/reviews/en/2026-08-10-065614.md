### Abstract: An Inspection Form Without a Signature

At six in the morning, the wet market still hangs in a thin mist. The inspector opens a drawer, pulls out a form, and checks the box marked "Pass." The vendor nods. Money changes hands. Everything looks orderly, like a ritual rehearsed countless times.

But once the ritual ends and the mist lifts, the market wakes—and the corners that went unseen begin to operate again.

This isn't a single case. It's the norm of an entire regulatory structure. When inspection becomes intermittent patrol, when the cost of violation falls below potential profit, and when merchants know the rule of the game is "inspect—comply—violate again," food safety degrades from a real guarantee into a piece of paper that can be signed.

### Clues: Why It Feels Wrong

Current food safety management relies on government inspection as its primary control mechanism. On paper, it sounds reasonable: someone checks, someone violates, someone gets punished. But look closer, and several key fractures appear.

Inspection is intermittent. It cannot achieve round-the-clock coverage, the way a guard can follow a schedule but not watch every door twenty-four hours a day. Merchants have incentive to lower quality standards in the gaps between inspections, because the cost of violation is often low relative to potential profit. A cycle forms: comply during inspection, revert afterward.

This isn't a moral problem. It's a problem of incentive structure. Merchants pursue profit maximization. Consumers lack sufficient information and choice. Information asymmetry means that when the market operates on its own, safety is often the first variable sacrificed.

### Technical Breakdown: Stripping the Story Back to Reality

The core contradiction of food safety regulation is this: inspection is a post-hoc mechanism, while risk is continuous.

When government can only rely on inspection to discover problems, it builds safety on the foundation of "being found" rather than "being ensured." This means that as long as inspection frequency isn't high enough and coverage isn't broad enough, risk will survive in the gaps of the system.

The more fundamental problem is that the current system hasn't changed merchants' cost-benefit calculation. When compliance costs less than the gains from violation, merchants have no incentive to proactively raise safety standards. Not because they don't know the risks—but because the market hasn't given them the right incentives.

### Another Path: Redistributing the Right to Choose

If inspection alone cannot eliminate risk, then changing the incentive structure may be the way forward.

The government could establish its own supply chain, directly controlling the source and circulation of ingredients. This isn't about replacing the market—it's about offering a clear, safe alternative. Even at a higher price, it lets consumers who can afford it make a choice.

The key to this approach isn't "supply." It's "signal." When the government provides a high-standard safety option, it sends a clear message to the market: food safety is a verifiable attribute, not something that depends solely on post-hoc inspection.

Consumers gain information transparency. A high-standard supply chain becomes a reference benchmark. Manufacturers, to maintain market share, must raise safety to a comparable level. Once the right to choose is redistributed, market pressure forms naturally.

### Limits and Boundaries

This model isn't unconditionally viable.

The operating cost of a government supply chain must be borne by taxpayers or consumers. A high-price strategy may benefit only certain groups, failing to cover everyone. When public power enters the market, there's also the risk of low efficiency or abuse of authority.

These limitations aren't reasons to dismiss the proposal—they're boundary conditions for implementation. No institutional design is perfect. What matters is whether it can generate the momentum for change within the existing structure.

### Auditor's Notes: What Comes Next

The root of the food safety problem isn't insufficiently strict inspection—it's an imbalanced incentive structure. Relying solely on checks cannot eliminate risk. Interests must be reallocated through market mechanisms.

A government-run supply chain as a lever is not a perfect solution, but it serves as an effective supplement to the current system. Its value lies not in fully replacing the market, but in offering choice—forcing manufacturers to reassess the trade-off between profit and safety.

For regulators, the next step is to evaluate the feasible boundaries of a government supply chain: cost structure, coverage scope, market impact. For consumers, understanding that choice exists is, in itself, a form of information.
