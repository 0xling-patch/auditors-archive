### Abstract: An Inspection Form Without a Signature

At six in the morning, the wet market still hung in a thin mist. The inspector opened a drawer, pulled out a form, and checked the box marked "Pass." The vendor nodded and tucked the envelope into his wallet. Everything looked orderly, like a ritual rehearsed countless times.

But once the ritual ended and the mist cleared, the market woke up—and the corners that had gone unseen began operating again.

This is not a single case. It is the norm of the entire regulatory structure. When inspection becomes intermittent patrol, when the cost of violation falls below potential profit, and when vendors know the rules of the game are "inspect—comply—violate again," food safety degrades from a substantive guarantee into a piece of paper that can be signed.

### Clues: Why It Feels Wrong

Current food safety management relies on government inspection as its primary control mechanism. The model sounds reasonable: someone inspects, someone violates, someone is penalized. But look closely, and several critical fractures appear.

Inspection is intermittent. It cannot achieve round-the-clock coverage, the way a guard can follow a schedule but cannot watch every door twenty-four hours a day. Vendors have incentive to lower quality standards in the gaps between inspections, because the cost of violation is often low relative to potential profit. A cycle forms: compliance during inspection, return to the status quo after.

This is not a moral problem. It is a problem of incentive structure. Merchants pursue profit maximization. Consumers lack sufficient information and choice. Information asymmetry means that when the market operates on its own, safety is often the first variable sacrificed.

### Technical Analysis: Dismantling the Story Back into Reality

The core contradiction of food safety regulation is this: inspection is a post-hoc mechanism, while risk is continuous.

When the government can only rely on inspection to discover problems, it builds safety on the foundation of "being found" rather than "being ensured." This means that as long as inspection frequency is insufficient and coverage is too narrow, risk will survive in the缝隙 of the system.

The more fundamental problem is that the current system does not change merchants' cost-benefit calculation. When compliance costs less than the returns from violation, merchants have no incentive to proactively raise safety standards. Not because they are unaware of the risk, but because the market has not given them the right incentives.

### Another Path: Redistributing the Right to Choose

If inspection alone cannot eradicate risk, then changing the incentive structure may be another way forward.

The government could establish its own supply chain, directly controlling the source and circulation of ingredients. This is not about replacing the market, but about offering a clear safety option—even at a higher price, it allows consumers with purchasing power to make a choice.

The key to this approach lies not in "supply" but in "signal." When the government provides a high-standard safety option, it sends a clear message to the market: food safety is an attribute that can be verified, not something that depends solely on post-hoc inspection.

Consumers gain information transparency. High-standard supply chains form a reference benchmark. Manufacturers, to maintain market share, must raise safety levels to a comparable standard. Once the right to choose is redistributed, market pressure forms naturally.

### Limitations and Boundaries

This model is not unconditionally feasible.

The operating costs of a government supply chain must be borne by taxpayers or consumers. A high-price strategy may benefit only certain groups and cannot achieve full coverage. When public power intervenes in the market, there is also a risk of low efficiency or abuse of authority.

These limitations are not reasons to dismiss the proposal, but boundary conditions for implementation. No institutional design is perfect. The question is whether it can generate the momentum for change within the existing structure.

### Auditor's Notes: Next Steps

The root of the food safety problem lies not in insufficiently strict inspection, but in an imbalanced incentive structure. Relying solely on inspection cannot eradicate risk. The distribution of interests must be reconfigured through market mechanisms.

A government-established supply chain, while not a perfect solution, serves as an effective supplement to the current system. Its value does not lie in fully replacing the market, but in providing choice—forcing manufacturers to reassess the trade-off between profit and safety.

For regulators, the next step is to evaluate the feasible boundaries of a government supply chain: cost structure, coverage scope, and market impact. For consumers, understanding that choice exists is, in itself, a form of information.
