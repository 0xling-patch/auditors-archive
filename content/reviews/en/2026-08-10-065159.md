### Abstract: An Inspection Form Without a Signature

At six in the morning, the wet market still hung in a thin mist. The inspector opened his drawer, pulled out a form, and checked the box marked "Pass." The vendor nodded. Money changed hands. Everything looked orderly—like a ritual rehearsed countless times.

But once the ritual ended and the fog lifted, once the market woke up, the unseen corners began operating again.

This isn't a single case. It's the norm of the entire regulatory structure. When inspections become intermittent patrols, when the cost of violation falls short of potential profit, when vendors know the rules of the game are "inspect—comply—violate again," food safety degrades from a real guarantee into a piece of paper that can be signed.

### Clues: Why It Feels Wrong

Current food safety management relies on government inspections as its primary control mechanism. On paper, it sounds reasonable: someone inspects, someone violates, someone gets punished. But look closely, and several key fractures appear.

Inspections are intermittent. They cannot achieve 24-hour coverage—like a guard who can only follow a schedule, unable to watch every door at all hours. Vendors have incentive to lower quality standards in the gaps between inspections, because the cost of violation is often low relative to potential profit. A cycle forms: compliance during inspection, return to the status quo after.

This isn't a moral problem. It's a problem of incentive structure. Merchants pursue profit maximization. Consumers lack sufficient information and choice. Information asymmetry means that when the market operates on its own, safety is often the first variable sacrificed.

### Technical Breakdown: Stripping the Story Back to Reality

The core contradiction in food safety regulation is this: inspections are a post-hoc mechanism, while risk is continuous.

When the government can only rely on inspections to discover problems, it builds safety on the foundation of "being found" rather than "being ensured." This means that as long as inspection frequency is insufficient and coverage is incomplete, risk will survive in the gaps of the system.

The more fundamental problem is that the current system hasn't changed merchants' cost-benefit calculations. When compliance costs less than the gains from violation, merchants have no incentive to proactively raise safety standards. Not because they don't know the risks—but because the market hasn't given them the right incentives.

### Another Path: Redistributing the Right to Choose

If inspections alone cannot eliminate risk, then changing the incentive structure may be the way forward.

The government could establish its own supply chain, directly controlling the source and circulation of ingredients. This isn't about replacing the market—it's about offering a clear safety option. Even at a higher price point, it allows consumers with purchasing power to make a choice.

The key to this approach isn't "supply"—it's "signal." When the government provides a high-standard safety option, it sends an unambiguous message to the market: food safety is a verifiable attribute, not something that depends solely on post-hoc inspection.

Consumers gain information transparency. High-standard supply chains become a reference benchmark. Manufacturers, to maintain market share, must raise safety levels to a comparable standard. Once the right to choose is redistributed, market pressure forms naturally.

### Limits and Boundaries

This model isn't unconditionally feasible.

The operating costs of a government supply chain must be borne by taxpayers or consumers. A high-price strategy may benefit only specific groups, failing to cover everyone. When public power intervenes in the market, there's also the risk of low efficiency or abuse of authority.

These limitations aren't reasons to dismiss the proposal—they're boundary conditions for implementation. No institutional design is perfect. The question is whether it can generate momentum for change within the existing structure.

### Auditor's Notes: What Comes Next

The root of the food safety problem isn't insufficiently strict inspections—it's an imbalanced incentive structure. Relying solely on checks cannot eliminate risk. Interests must be reallocated through market mechanisms.

A government-run supply chain, used as a lever, isn't a perfect solution. But it serves as an effective supplement to the current system. Its value lies not in fully replacing the market, but in providing choice—forcing manufacturers to reassess the trade-off between profit and safety.

For regulators, the next step is to evaluate the feasible boundaries of a government supply chain: cost structure, coverage scope, market impact. For consumers, understanding that choice exists is, in itself, a form of information.
